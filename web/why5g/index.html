Why private 5G + edge computing for remote rescue robots?

A rescue rover is driven from a VR headset over the lab's private 5G network, with the safety layer and the AI running on an edge server (MEC) next to the 5G core. Below: what the robot needs, why 5G provides it, and a side-by-side comparison measured live in this demo.

What a teleoperated rescue robot needs from the network

Low, predictable latencyto steer precisely and to stop in time
High uplink bandwidthlive camera video flows up from the robot
Reliabilitya stalled link means a robot with nobody in control
Local processingAI and safety decisions close to the robot
Coverage & mobilitythe robot moves across a whole disaster site
Securitya private network, not the open internet

Why private 5G + MEC each card points to where this demo shows it

URLLC · low latency

The robot stops in time

5G schedules the radio in fractions of a millisecond, and with the server at the edge there is no internet round trip. Every millisecond at 29 km/h is 8 mm driven blind.

Demo: “Person crosses road” stop test, chart below
MEC · edge computing

AI and safety next to the radio

The local UPF breaks traffic out straight to the edge server, so detection and the stop decision happen within the site instead of in a far-away cloud region.

Demo: AI stop latency = 5G round trip + inference only
eMBB · high uplink

Video from the robot

5G TDD can be configured for heavy uplink, which suits robots that stream camera video to the operator and to the AI. Wi-Fi and 4G uplinks fill up much sooner.

Full project: 720p camera stream over WebRTC
QoS · network slicing

Control traffic gets priority

5G can give control and safety messages a guaranteed quality-of-service class or their own slice, so a phone uploading video next to the robot cannot starve them, unlike on shared Wi-Fi.

Demo: compare with the “Busy Wi-Fi” profile (spikes, stalls)
Private network

Secure and on-site

SIM-based authentication and a private core: only registered devices join, and the robot's data never leaves the site.

Lab: Niral 5G core, local breakout to the MEC
Mobility & coverage

One network across the site

Cells cover hundreds of metres to kilometres, with seamless handover as the robot moves. Wi-Fi access points cover tens of metres and drop connections when roaming.

Architecture

How the comparison works: the rover, safety layer and AI really run on the edge server, and the headset really talks to it over the lab's private 5G. The other networks are emulated on top of that real link by adding their typical latency, jitter and packet-loss stalls (TCP-like: in order, a lost packet stalls everything behind it). The values are illustrative and live in server/netctl.py.
